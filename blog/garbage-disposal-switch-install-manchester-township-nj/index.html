Garbage Disposal Switch Installation: Manchester Township, NJ

A disposal switch is an electrical part, so the install is usually split between an electrician and a plumber. Here is how the options work and who does what.

By Manchester Township Express PlumbingPublished October 13, 2026Updated October 13, 2026

The short answer on installing a disposal switch

Installing a switch for a garbage disposal is mostly electrical work. The switch controls power to the motor, and that wiring ties into your home's circuit. In New Jersey, a licensed electrician may be required for new wiring, and permit rules vary by town.

Our role is the plumbing side. That means mounting the disposal under the sink, connecting the drain and dishwasher line, and testing for leaks. If the disposal itself is failing, our garbage disposal repair service covers the unit.

Safety first: power off at the breaker

Before anyone touches a disposal, a switch box or the wires near the sink, turn off the circuit at the breaker panel. Flipping the wall switch off is not enough. A switch can fail, and a disposal can start when it is unplugged from nothing but a loose connection. Never put a hand in the disposal, even when it looks off.

Wall switch or air switch: what each one is

Most kitchens use one of two switch types. A standard wall switch sits on the backsplash wall or beside the sink and cuts power with a flip. An air switch is a small button mounted on the countertop or sink rim that sends a puff of air through a tube to a controller under the sink.

Both do the same job. They differ in where the wiring goes, how much counter space they take, and how easy they are to reach with wet hands. The table below compares them in plain terms.

Wall switch compared with air switch
FeatureWall switchAir switch
Where it mountsWall, near the sinkCountertop or sink rim
How it worksOpens and closes the circuit directlyAir pulse triggers a controller under the sink
Outlet neededSwitched outlet or hardwired boxOutlet for the controller, usually under the sink
SuitsKitchens with an existing switched circuitKitchens with no wall space near the sink
Who typically handles itElectricianElectrician or a qualified installer

Always follow the manufacturer's instructions for your disposal model.

What the plumbing side of the job includes

  1. Confirm the unit and sinkCheck that the disposal fits your sink flange and that the sink has a standard 3.5 inch drain opening.
  2. Disconnect powerThe circuit is shut off at the breaker panel before any old unit is removed.
  3. Remove the old disposalThe discharge pipe, dishwasher hose and mounting ring come off. This is where leaks often start.
  4. Mount the new unitThe mounting assembly clamps to the sink flange and the unit locks into place.
  5. Reconnect drain and dishwasher lineThe discharge tube joins the trap. The dishwasher knockout plug is removed if a dishwasher drains into the unit.
  6. Test for leaksWater runs for several minutes with the power restored by the electrician, and every joint is checked dry.

If you want the whole project priced as one visit, tell dispatch about the switch when you call. We can coordinate the plumbing portion and let you know where a licensed electrician needs to take over. See our general plumbing installation page for what we install.

What you can do yourself and when to call

Homeowners can safely do a few things. You can clear the area under the sink, check for drips at the trap, and read the model label so the right switch kit is chosen. You can also test the reset button on the bottom of the unit after the power is off at the breaker.

  • Do: photograph the label on the disposal and the switch plate before calling.
  • Do: run cold water while using the disposal and for 15 seconds after.
  • Do not: open a switch box or splice wires without the right license and training.
  • Do not: use a disposal that hums but does not spin. Turn off the breaker and call.
  • Call a plumber for drips, slow draining through the disposal, or a loose mounting ring.
  • Call an electrician for a dead switch, a tripped breaker that keeps tripping, or missing power at the outlet.

Disposal switches in Manchester Township homes

Census data shows a median home build year of 1979 in Manchester Township, and about 52 percent of homes date before 1980. In an older kitchen, the sink cabinet may have no switched outlet at all. Ask about this before buying a disposal, because adding a circuit is the larger cost item.

Some Manchester Township homes are on public sewer and some are on septic. Disposals send extra solids into a septic tank, so a septic home should check with its septic service before adding one. If you are not sure which system your home uses, ask before you assume.

Township water is moderately soft to soft. Manchester Township Water Utility reports 2025 hardness of roughly 52 to 96 ppm across its two service areas. That means mineral scale inside the disposal is mild, but food residue and grease still build up, so keep running cold water.

Permits and inspections for electrical and plumbing work go through the municipal construction office under New Jersey's Uniform Construction Code. Call that office before a new circuit goes in, since permit rules vary by town and by job.

Questions to settle before you buy a switch kit

A few quick checks can save a second trip to the hardware store. Does the disposal come with a cord, or is it hardwired? Is there a switched outlet under the sink already? Is the countertop thick stone that cannot be drilled easily?

If the answers are uncertain, an air switch kit may avoid cutting the wall. Still, the controller needs power under the sink, so the electrical question comes back either way. Book both trades for the same day when you can, and our dispatch can note this on the call.

Choosing a spot for the switch

Placement affects daily use. A wall switch should sit where you can reach it without leaning over the sink, and away from splash. An air switch button should sit on a dry, flat part of the counter or sink rim.

Kids and guests matter too. A switch placed too close to the faucet gets bumped on and off. Think about who uses the kitchen and keep the control out of the way of cutting boards and dish racks.

Whatever you pick, label the breaker for the disposal circuit. A labeled panel makes a future repair faster and safer for everyone who works in the kitchen.

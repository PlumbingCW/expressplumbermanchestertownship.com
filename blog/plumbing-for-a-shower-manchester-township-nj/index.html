How Shower Plumbing Works: A Manchester Township, NJ Guide

A shower needs four things behind the wall: a mixing valve, hot and cold supply pipes, a drain with a trap, and a vent. Here is how they fit together.

By Manchester Township Express PlumbingPublished October 13, 2026Updated October 13, 2026

Four systems behind one shower wall

Every shower, from a basic tub surround to a tiled walk-in, uses the same plumbing logic. Water arrives through two supply pipes, one hot and one cold. A valve blends them, a showerhead delivers the mix, and the used water falls into a drain that includes a trap and connects to a vent.

Understanding that layout helps you plan a remodel, talk to a contractor, and spot trouble early. This guide covers each part in the order water travels, then explains the rough-in stage that comes before tile goes up.

If you are replacing a fixture and want a pro to run the pipes, plumbing installation is the service to call about. Dispatch at (609) 547-1417 is open 24/7.

The path of water, part by part

Supply lines

Hot water comes from the water heater and cold water from the main. Both run to the shower valve, usually in half-inch pipe. Shutoff valves, sometimes called stops, are installed upstream so you can work on the shower without turning off the whole house.

The mixing valve

The valve is the control. A pressure-balancing valve keeps the temperature steady when someone flushes a toilet, which helps prevent sudden scalding. A thermostatic valve lets you set an exact temperature and is common in larger, multi-head showers.

Showerhead and arm

The riser pipe carries mixed water up to the head. A bracket inside the wall holds the shower arm firm so it does not wobble or loosen over time.

Drain, trap and vent

The drain collects water in the shower pan. Below it sits a trap, a curved section of pipe that holds a small plug of water. That plug blocks sewer gas from rising into the bathroom. The vent lets air enter behind the water so the drain flows smoothly instead of gurgling.

Rules that shape a shower

Shower valves, drains and traps must follow New Jersey's Uniform Construction Code plumbing subcode (N.J.A.C. 5:23-3.15), and the federal flow standard for showerheads is 2.5 gallons per minute, with EPA WaterSense labeled heads rated at 2.0. Check both before you buy fixtures.

A worn cartridge is the most common reason a shower drips or fights back on temperature.

Rough-in: the stage before tile

Rough-in is when pipes are set inside open walls and the floor, before the shower pan, backer board and tile are installed. It is the right time to fix anything, because changes later mean tearing out finished surfaces. Plumbers set the valve at a comfortable height, run the supply and drain, and pressure test the lines.

  1. Decide the layout: valve location, number of heads, and drain position.
  2. Open the wall or floor and confirm the studs and joists can hold the new pipes.
  3. Run the hot and cold supply to the valve body and the riser to the head.
  4. Set the drain and trap, connect to the vent, and slope the line correctly.
  5. Pressure test the supply and water test the drain before closing anything.
  6. Schedule any required inspection before the walls are covered.

Keep the inspection window open

Inspectors look at rough plumbing before it is hidden. If you cover the walls first, you may be asked to open them again. Ask the municipal construction office when to call for inspection.

What a homeowner can do and when to call

  • Cartridge replacementWith the water off, many single-handle valves allow a swap of the cartridge, but the exact part must match.
  • Showerhead cleaningSoaking the head in vinegar loosens mineral scale and restores spray.
  • Hair catcherA cheap drain strainer prevents most clogs before they begin.

Call a plumber for anything inside the wall, a new valve location, a drain that must be moved, or a shower that gurgles. A single-handle faucet that will not stop dripping after a new cartridge is often the case for faucet repair. A tub or shower drain that stays slow after cleaning needs a cable or drain cleaning to reach past the trap.

Never skip the trap

A shower without a working trap lets sewer gas into the room. If a bathroom smells like rotten eggs, check for a dry trap in a rarely used shower, then run water for a minute to refill it.

Manchester Township: what the local details change

Housing age shapes what is behind your shower wall. Manchester's median home was built in 1979, and about a third of all units date to the 1970s. That puts many showers at or past the age when valve cartridges, fixtures and old supply connections wear out. If your remodel opens a wall, replace the valve and shutoffs while the wall is open.

Water quality is mild here. The Manchester Township Water Utility's 2025 data put hardness at about 3 to 5 grains per gallon in the eastern system and 4 to 6 in the western system around Whiting. That is soft to moderately soft, so scale is a slower problem than in many places. A showerhead still collects deposits, but cleaning it a couple of times a year is usually enough.

Iron is the one to watch. In the western system it reached about 1 ppm in 2025, above the 0.3 ppm taste-and-staining level. If you see orange tint on shower tile or fixtures, tell your plumber, because it may affect which finish or filter makes sense.

Permits and inspections for plumbing work go through the township's municipal construction office under the Uniform Construction Code. Ask which work needs a permit before ordering materials. If you are weighing a plumber in a nearby town, such as Toms River or Lakewood, remember that each town runs its own construction office.
